Orbit algebra

The smallest rules known on most domains are symmetric: they are unchanged by the symmetry group of the domain. The package uses that structure both to search for such rules and to refine them. This page works through the triangle, the tetrahedron, the sphere and the box; the same construction carries over to the other domains of the seed tables, with the differences listed at the end.

Orbits

A symmetric rule consists of whole orbits: a point together with all its images under the group, sharing one weight. A rule is then described by a list of orbit types and, for each orbit, a weight and a few parameters that fix the representative point. This is the structure of the rule, and the parameters are the unknowns that the refinement solves for.

Simplices

On a D-simplex the group is S_{D+1}, which permutes the barycentric coordinates. An orbit type is a pattern of equal coordinates. On the triangle there are three:

TypeRepresentativePointsParameters
[3](1/3, 1/3, 1/3)10
[2,1](a, a, 1-2a)31
[1,1,1](a, b, 1-a-b)62

The tetrahedron has five orbit types under S₄, from the centroid (1 point) to the general orbit (24 points).

The sphere

On the sphere in ℝ³ the group is the octahedral group O_h: the 48 signed permutations of the coordinates. Its orbits are the six used by Lebedev:

The square and the cube

On [-1, 1]^D the group is again the signed permutations of the coordinates: 8 on the square, 48 on the cube. An orbit is fixed by its representative's distinct nonzero absolute values, how often each is repeated, and how many coordinates are zero. The square has four types and the cube seven:

The moment equations

A rule made of whole orbits is invariant under the group. Its integration error on a polynomial p therefore equals its error on the group average of p. Every polynomial whose group average is zero is integrated exactly, whatever the parameters are. So only the invariant polynomials give equations, and there are far fewer of them than polynomials of the same degree.

On simplices

The equations are projected onto the invariant subspace of each degree block of an orthonormal basis. That subspace is the range of the Reynolds (group-averaging) operator, and its dimension is checked against the Molien series of the group. The resulting system has exactly as many equations as independent invariant moments.

The invariant basis is computed in Float64. An inexact basis only affects the conditioning, not the solution, because the residual of a symmetric rule already lies in the invariant subspace.

On the sphere

The invariant polynomials of O_h on ℝ³ are generated by p₂ = Σxᵢ², p₄ = Σxᵢ⁴ and p₆ = x²y²z². On the sphere p₂ = 1, so the invariant polynomials of degree at most n are spanned by p₄^a p₆^b with 4a + 6b ≤ n. At degree 131 that gives a few hundred equations, where the full set of spherical harmonics would give 17 424.

Because an invariant polynomial has the same value at every point of an orbit, each orbit only needs to be evaluated at its representative. With the constraint substituted, p₄ and p₆ are polynomials in the orbit parameters, so the equations contain no square roots, and their exact integrals are rationals. The searches use these equations, in Float64, and the certificate of a refined rule quotes its residual in them.

The refinement solves the same equations in another basis. The products p₄^a p₆^b behave like monomials: the system's condition number is about 10⁵⁴ at degree 133 and 10⁸¹ at 201, and above about 10¹⁰ a Newton step can no longer use a Float64 factorisation and must factor in BigFloat. So the equations are rewritten in the orthonormal O_h-invariant spherical harmonics (OctahedralHarmonicSystem). A combination of P̄_ℓ^m(cos θ) cos mφ with ℓ even and m ≡ 0 (mod 4) is invariant under D₄ₕ, the 16 symmetries that keep the z-axis, whatever its coefficients. Averaged over the rotations x → y → z → x it is O_h-invariant, again exactly: D₄ₕ, D₄ₕR and D₄ₕR² are the cosets of D₄ₕ in O_h. The coefficients that make the averages orthonormal are computed once, in Float64; their rounding affects only the conditioning, never which parameters solve the equations, which are the same unknowns with the same solutions. Every harmonic of degree ℓ > 0 integrates to zero, so there are no moments to compute, and the condition number stays moderate: about 500 at degree 59, 3·10³ at 133 and 3·10⁵ at 189.

Every orbit is centrally symmetric, so odd-degree polynomials integrate to zero for any parameters. A rule exact to degree 2k is therefore exact to 2k + 1, and Lebedev rules are only offered at odd degrees.

On the square and the cube

The invariant polynomials are the symmetric polynomials in x₁², …, x_D². Here no invariant basis has to be computed: the symmetrised products of even-degree orthonormal Legendre polynomials,

\[\varphi_\alpha = c_\alpha \sum_\sigma p_{\alpha_{\sigma(1)}}(x_1) \cdots p_{\alpha_{\sigma(D)}}(x_D), \qquad \alpha_1 \ge \dots \ge \alpha_D \ge 0 \text{ all even},\]

summed over the distinct rearrangements of α, are already orthonormal on the box. Only the constant has a nonzero integral, the system is as well conditioned as the rule itself allows, and each orbit is again evaluated at its representative only. As on the sphere, a rule of degree 2k is one of degree 2k + 1, so only odd degrees are tabulated.

Conditioning

The basis p₄^a p₆^b is badly conditioned, for the same reason monomials are: the condition number of its Gram matrix grows by about 0.83 decades per degree (about 10¹¹ at degree 17, 10²¹ at degree 29). That is why the refinement on the sphere works in the orthonormal invariant harmonics instead (On the sphere). Orthonormalising the products through a Cholesky factor of their Gram matrix, tried first, did not help: the residual was still formed in the old basis, so the working precision stayed as high (notes/v0.4-spheres.md). On every domain the equations the refinement solves are now in an orthonormal basis, and the condition number in a certificate is that of the rule's own parameters, not of the basis.

How large the systems are

For the shipped rules the orbit parameters and the invariant equations nearly match one for one: most systems are square, some have a few unknowns to spare. The Jacobians are dense. The number of unknowns is the number of invariants, which grows asymptotically like the number of basis functions divided by the order of the group: d²/12 on the triangle and d³/144 on the tetrahedron, a sixth and a twenty-fourth of the moment equations a rule without symmetry would have to satisfy. That reduction, together with the moderate conditioning, is what makes refinement to hundreds of digits affordable. In October 2026 a 200-digit rule took under a second for the triangle at degree 20, about 2 s at degree 30 and about 20 s at degree 50; the tetrahedron at degree 20 took about 20 s. The Lebedev systems are as small (352 unknowns at degree 125), and in the invariant harmonics as well conditioned.

Recovering the structure of a rule

CubatureRules.classify_octahedral(points, weights) decomposes a set of points on the sphere into O_h orbits, or reports that this is impossible. This is how a rule from another source, such as Lebedev.jl, is converted into orbit parameters so it can be refined.

Other domains

The same construction serves every domain of the seed tables: a rule of whole orbits of the domain's symmetry group, one equation per invariant polynomial, written in an orthonormal invariant basis so that only the constant has a nonzero integral. What changes from domain to domain is the group, the orbit types and where the invariant basis comes from.

DomainGroupOrbitsInvariant basis
4-simplexS₅, 120 elementsas on the triangle, in five barycentric coordinatesprojected from the orthonormal Dubiner polynomials, as on the triangle
4-cubeB₄, 384 elementsas on the cube, in four coordinatessymmetrised products of even Legendre polynomials, as on the cube
diskD₄, 8 elementsthose of the square, inside the unit diskZernike polynomials p_k(2r² − 1) Re (x + iy)^m with m ≡ 0 (mod 4), already invariant
ballOₕ, 48 elementsthose of the cube, inside the unit ballp_k(2r² − 1) r^ℓ F(x̂), with F an Oₕ-invariant harmonic of the sphere
pyramidC₄ᵥ, 8 elementsa square orbit at a height zproducts in the collapsed coordinates x/(1 − z), y/(1 − z) and z
wedgeD₃ₕ, 12 elementsa triangle orbit at z = 0 or at the pair ±zthe triangle's invariants times even Legendre polynomials in z

On the disk and the ball the radial factor p_k is the orthonormal Jacobi polynomial for (1 + t)^(ℓ + D/2 − 1), which makes the products orthonormal on the unit ball: r^ℓ F(x̂) is a harmonic polynomial, so each product is a polynomial of degree 2k + ℓ. On the pyramid the collapsed coordinates turn every cross-section into the square [-1, 1]², and the weight (1 − z)^(2(i + j) + 2) in z absorbs the Jacobian, so the products are orthonormal there. The searches on all of these domains share one multistart → grow → eliminate loop, written against the small interface each orbit structure implements (src/refine/orbit_search.jl).
