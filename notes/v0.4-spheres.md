# v0.4 — spheres: what was decided and why

Companion to `notes/v0.0-spike.md` and `notes/v0.2-tetrahedra.md`. This one records the
design choices behind the sphere domain and the plan for Lebedev, so that the reasoning is
not buried in commit messages.

## The domain

`Sphere{D}` is the `(D-1)`-sphere embedded in `R^D`: `Sphere{3}()` is the familiar surface,
`Sphere{2}()` the circle. The type parameter counts *coordinates*, matching every other
domain in the package, rather than the dimension of the surface. The alternative — indexing
by the surface dimension, so `Sphere{2}` is the 2-sphere — reads better in isolation and
worse everywhere else, since `Domain{D,T}` means "points have D coordinates" throughout.

Two consequences had to be handled explicitly.

**A sphere has no boundary.** `isinterior` is therefore `indomain`, not something stricter.
Without that, the registry's `interior = true` filter rejects every rule on a sphere, which
is the opposite of the truth: no node can be on a boundary that does not exist.

**`|x| = 1` is never exact.** Both predicates carry a tolerance defaulting to the resolution
of the coordinate type rather than to zero, and a rule's certificate records the largest
deviation of `|x|` from 1 over its nodes, which is the defining equation of the domain in
the same sense that the moment system is for a simplex rule.

## Verification: harmonics, not monomials

Monomials restricted to a sphere are linearly *dependent* — `Σxᵢ² = 1` — so they are not a
basis at all, and a Gram–Schmidt over them collapses. The real spherical harmonics of
degree ≤ d span exactly the polynomials of degree ≤ d restricted to the sphere, which is
what a degree claim on a sphere means, so they are the basis the rules are verified in.

Evaluated by the fully normalised recurrence, never by factorials: `(l+m)!` overflows
`Float64` by `l = 85`, and the rules this is meant to check reach degree 131 and beyond.

The one compromise is the **gradient**. The verification tolerance is
`16 ε Σ|wᵢ| (|φ| + |∇φ| · max(|x|, 1))`, so it needs a gradient magnitude. The exact
surface gradient of `Y_l^m` needs the `P_l^m / sin θ` recurrence to stay finite at the
poles; rather than carry that, the basis reports the rigorous bound
`√(l(l+1)) · √((2l+1)/4π)`, which is the Bernstein inequality times the sup norm. The
tolerance is then conservative by a factor growing like `l` — about one digit of slack at
degree 20. That can only make a marginal rule fail, never let a wrong one pass. If the
slack ever matters, the fix is the stable recurrence, not a looser bound.

## Product rules

Gauss–Legendre in `cos θ` against the trapezoid rule in `φ`. The trapezoid rule is
spectrally exact on a circle, so `m = d+1` points in `φ` and `n = ⌈(d+1)/2⌉` in `t` give
degree `d` in `⌈(d+1)/2⌉(d+1)` points — roughly twice a minimal rule, and available at
every degree and precision with no table. This is the sphere's `ConicalProduct`: the thing
the selector can always fall back on.

On the circle the trapezoid rule alone is optimal, `d+1` points for degree `d`, so there is
nothing further to want there.

## Lebedev: the moment system to build next

The obstacle is that the existing orbit machinery is `S_N` acting on barycentric
coordinates, and Lebedev rules are invariant under the octahedral group `O_h` — all
permutations of coordinates times all sign changes, 48 elements. Its orbits on `S²` are the
classical six:

| orbit | representative | points | free parameters |
|---|---|---|---|
| a₁ | `(1,0,0)` | 6 | — |
| a₂ | `(1,1,0)/√2` | 12 | — |
| a₃ | `(1,1,1)/√3` | 8 | — |
| b(l) | `(l,l,m)`, `m = √(1-2l²)` | 24 | 1 |
| c(p) | `(p,q,0)`, `q = √(1-p²)` | 24 | 1 |
| d(r,s) | general | 48 | 2 |

each contributing one weight besides.

**The equations.** A rule built from whole orbits is `O_h`-invariant by construction, so its
residual against any harmonic whose group average vanishes is *identically* zero — no
information, and at degree 131 that is 17424 equations of which only a few hundred say
anything. The reduced system should therefore be written directly in the invariants rather
than projected out of the harmonics afterwards.

The invariant ring of `O_h` on `R³` is free on

    p₂ = x² + y² + z²,   p₄ = x⁴ + y⁴ + z⁴,   p₆ = x²y²z².

On the sphere `p₂ = 1`, so the invariant polynomials of degree ≤ n restricted to `S²` are
spanned by `p₄^a p₆^b` with `4a + 6b ≤ n` — about `n²/48` functions, which is the right
count. The moment equations are then

    Σᵢ wᵢ p₄(xᵢ)^a p₆(xᵢ)^b = ∫_{S²} p₄^a p₆^b dσ,

whose right-hand sides come from the exact monomial moments already implemented, by
multinomial expansion.

That basis is not orthonormal and will condition badly at high degree, so the intended step
before solving is a Gram–Schmidt against the sphere inner product — a few hundred squared
entries, each an exact moment sum, which is cheap next to the refinement itself. The
existing rank-revealing solve and guard-digit machinery then apply unchanged.

**Check before trusting any of it.** The claim that `{p₄^a p₆^b : 4a+6b ≤ n}` spans the
invariants, and is independent, should be verified numerically against the harmonic-side
dimension count (the Molien series `1/((1-t²)(1-t⁴)(1-t⁶))`) before it is built on. That
check belongs in the test suite, not in a comment.

## What the Lebedev search found

Implemented as planned above: orbits, invariants `p₄^a p₆^b`, one equation per invariant,
Levenberg–Marquardt then Gauss–Newton from random admissible parameters, point counts walked
upward until something positive and non-degenerate appears. The seeds are in
`src/data/lebedev_seeds.toml` and nothing was copied from any table.

The counts reproduce Lebedev's — 6, 14, 26, 38, 50, 86, 110 for degrees 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15,
17 — with one exception that is worth recording, because it is a property of the problem
rather than of the search.

**Degree 13.** Lebedev's rule has 74 points; ours has 78. At 74 points there are exactly
four admissible structures, and they were all tried exhaustively:

| structure | unknowns | equations | result over 8000 starts |
|---|---|---|---|
| a₁ a₂ a₃ d | 6 | 7 | no solution converged |
| a₁ a₂ a₃ b b | 7 | 7 | no solution converged |
| a₁ a₂ a₃ b c | 7 | 7 | 218 solutions, best minimum weight **−0.372** |
| a₁ a₂ a₃ c c | 7 | 7 | no solution converged |

So the 74-point degree-13 rule exists and is not positive, which is the known character of
that entry in Lebedev's table. Our search requires positive weights, so it reports the
smallest positive rule instead, 78 points with structure a₁ b b c. This is not a
disagreement about the mathematics, only about what is being asked for — and it is the
reason the family's documentation states the count as "Lebedev's, except 78 at degree 13".

As always, a multistart search is not a proof: "no solution converged" means none was found
in 8000 starts, not that none exists.

**On the conditioning**, the plan above expected the raw `p₄^a p₆^b` basis to need
orthonormalising before it could be solved in. So far it has not: the measured `cond(J)`
runs from 1 at degree 3 to 6e5 at degree 17, which the existing guard-digit machinery
absorbs without comment. The Gram–Schmidt step remains the thing to reach for if the
condition number becomes the limit at high degree, rather than something to add pre-emptively.

## Where the search stops, and why

Pure multistart reproduces Lebedev's counts to degree 17 and then stalls. At degree 19 it
walked past 146 points — the published count, and a positive rule — and reported a
206-point rule instead. That entry was removed rather than shipped: a rule 41% larger than
the known one is not what `rule(Sphere{3}(); degree = 19)` should hand over.

The cause is not the moment system but the seeding. Degree 19 needs 12 invariant equations,
so the structures that can satisfy it have twelve-dimensional parameter spaces, and random
starts land in the right basin too rarely at 2048 tries per structure. This is the same wall
the triangle table hit at degree 20, and the fix there was the same one that should be
applied here: **grow from the degree below** rather than starting cold. The degree-17 rule
has structure a₁ a₃ b b b c at 110 points; 146 is that plus a₂ and one more 24-point orbit,
which is a two-orbit growth step from a good starting point rather than a cold search in
twelve dimensions.

Until that exists, the shipped table stops at degree 17 and `SphereProduct` covers
everything above it — more nodes, but available at every degree and verified the same way.

One wording bug was fixed at the same time. The generator annotated any count above the
published one as "more than the published count, which is not positive", which was written
for degree 13 and is an unjustified claim anywhere else: exceeding the published count means
*this search* did not reproduce that rule. Whether a published rule is positive is settled by
an exhaustive run at its own count, as was done at degree 13, not by a walk that stops at the
first success.

## Growing from the degree below: what it fixed, and what it did not

The grow-then-eliminate machinery now exists (`src/refine/octahedral_grow.jl`), mirroring
`refine/elimination.jl` for simplices, and it closes part of the gap.

**It works where multistart needed a walk.** Growing the 86-point degree-15 rule reaches the
110-point degree-17 rule — Lebedev's own count — in about a second, by adding a single
24-point orbit and refitting. Before, that count was only found by walking point counts
upward from 6.

Two things had to differ from the simplex version, both because sphere degrees step by two
rather than one:

*Added orbits must carry real mass.* The simplex `grow` adds orbits at 2% of a typical
weight, which is fine when the rule below misses only the top moments slightly. Here the
rule two degrees down misses the new invariants by O(0.3), and a 2% orbit cannot absorb
that: the fit stalls around 1e-3 and the new orbit slides into an existing one instead. At
half the mean weight it converges.

*A square system is tried first.* The recipes now enumerate the cheapest orbit additions
that bring the unknown count up to the number of equations, rather than overshooting by a
fixed margin. Overshooting by even one unknown skipped the addition that actually works —
86 points plus one 24-point orbit is exactly 110.

**Degree 19 is still out of reach**, and the evidence says it is a search problem rather
than a defect. Every structure with the right count was tried from the degree-17 rule — a
single b or c orbit (134 points), a₂ with b or c (146, which is Lebedev's own count and
structure), two b orbits, a d orbit — at 48 starts each, with jitter on the inherited
parameters from 0 to 35%, and with continuation in the new equations. All stall at a
residual around 3e-6 with *healthy* margins: separation 0.17, weights positive, nothing
degenerate. That is a genuine local minimum of the least-squares problem, not a rejected
rule.

What is left to try, roughly in order of expected value: basin hopping from the stall point
rather than restarting cold; a trust-region solver that handles rank deficiency better than
Levenberg–Marquardt with a plain accept test; and simply many more starts at the two
promising counts. Failing all of that, degree 19 and above are exactly where an explicit
seed from someone who already has the numbers is worth more than any search.

## The condition-number diagnostic was most of the refinement cost

Refining Lebedev order 125 to 100 digits took 1701.9 s for 6 Gauss–Newton iterations on a
352 × 352 system. Profiling the pieces at that size and conditioning (σ₁/σ_min ≈ 1e54, which
is what the run reported):

| per iteration | cost |
|---|---|
| pivoted QR at 560 bits — the actual solve | 52.8 s |
| `svdvals` at 256 bits — the condition estimate | 86.6 s |

`lsq_step` called `estimate_cond` every iteration, and `estimate_cond` falls back to a
BigFloat SVD whenever the Float64 copy cannot resolve the condition number — which is always
true for a system like this one. So the diagnostic cost 1.6× the solve it accompanied. Worse,
`gauss_newton` ended with a second full `lsq_step` purely to read κ off it, discarding the
step: seven factorisation pairs for six iterations.

The number is not decoration — `guard_bits_from_cond(cond_max)` decides whether
`refine_octahedral` re-runs with more guard, and for this system the true 9.01e54 asks for
~182 guard bits where a saturated Float64 estimate would ask for ~53. It cannot be dropped.
It can be sampled: the Jacobian of a Gauss–Newton run varies smoothly, so the accurate
estimate is now taken at the first and last iterate and the cheap `|R₁₁|/|R_rr|` ratio from
the pivoted QR — already computed for the rank test, so free — is used in between. The
trailing `lsq_step` became a bare `estimate_cond`.

Six iterations therefore cost 6 QR + 2 SVD rather than 7 QR + 7 SVD, which on the component
timings above is 486 s saved of 1702 — about 29%. The rest is Jacobian assembly and the
line search, which evaluate `F(θ)` more than once per iteration. (29%, not the ~50% guessed
before measuring: the linear algebra is a smaller share of the whole than it looked.)

One trap on the way: the free estimate over the *retained* columns reports κ = 1 for a
rank-deficient Jacobian, which is flattering nonsense and would pick minimal guard digits.
It now reaches one entry past the truncation — the entry that failed the rank test — so
degeneracy still reports a large number. `test_refine.jl` caught this immediately, which is
the test earning its keep.

Float64 paths are untouched: `lsq_step`'s Float64 method already had the SVD in hand and
still returns σ₁/σ_min, so every seed search behaves exactly as before.

## Orthonormalising the invariant basis: measured, and not adopted

The raw invariant basis `p₄^i p₆^j` is nearly linearly dependent, for the same reason
monomials are. Measured through the Cholesky diagonal of its Gram matrix
`G[a,b] = ∫ p₄^(iₐ+i_b) p₆^(jₐ+j_b) dσ`:

| degree | conditions | log₁₀ cond(G) |
|---|---|---|
| 17 | 10 | 11 |
| 29 | 24 | 21 |

about 0.83 decades per degree, so ≈ 10¹⁰¹ by degree 125 — and `√cond(G) ≈ 10⁵⁰` against the
`cond(J) = 9.01e54` actually measured there. The conditioning is entirely an artefact of how
the equations are written, not a property of the rule.

Restating them against an orthonormal basis of the same span is one Cholesky and a triangular
solve (`L \ r`, `L \ J`). Implemented and measured:

| degree | cond before | cond after | guard before | guard after | time before | time after |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11 | 1.53e3 | 5.04e0 | 14 | 12 | — | — |
| 17 | 5.98e5 | 2.32e1 | 16 | 12 | — | — |
| 35 | 5.23e13 | 1.01e3 | 24 | 26 | 15.5 s | 16.2 s |
| 47 | 1.57e19 | 3.39e4 | 28 | 31 | 6.9 s | 10.7 s |
| 59 | 5.05e24 | 1.39e6 | 36 | 38 | 16.9 s | 17.7 s |

Ten to eighteen orders of conditioning, and it still lost. **Reverted.**

The reason is worth keeping, because it is not obvious and it cost three wrong turns to find.
Orthonormalising fixes the linear *solve*. It does not fix the arithmetic that feeds it: the
residual `r_c = Σ_q W_q p_c(x_q) − M_c` is still formed in the raw basis, and rotating it
afterwards rotates its rounding noise too. `L \ r` cannot go below `‖L⁻¹‖ = 1/min Lᵢᵢ` times
that noise, and `‖L⁻¹‖ ≈ √cond(G)` is precisely the amplification the raw `cond(J)` was
already reporting. Guard digits have to cover it either way.

The three wrong turns, in order:

1. Leaving `res_floor` at `2^-(wbits-12)` made it unreachable in the new norm, and
   Gauss–Newton ground on past convergence — three iterations became eight at degree 47, for
   no gain in accuracy.
2. Scaling the floor by the amplification made it *too* loose, and degree 59 came back
   claiming 50 digits while verification found 1.47e-45. The floor is a noise threshold, and
   getting it wrong in either direction is worse than not having moved it.
3. Taking guard digits from the preconditioned condition number alone under-guarded by the
   whole amplification factor; using `κ × amp` restored correctness but over-estimated what
   the raw `cond(J)` measures directly, so guard went *up* by two or three digits.

Fixing all three left the answer identical (the residuals match to the digit), the iteration
count identical, and the wall time worse by the Gram Cholesky and a triangular solve per
iteration.

Where it *does* pay is a search rather than a polish. A well-scaled merit function matters
enormously when comparing candidates and running a line search over thousands of trial
structures, which is the setting it was borrowed from. Our refinement starts from a seed
that is already correct to Float64 and converges in three Newton steps, so a better-behaved
merit function buys nothing. If the multistart search in `octahedral_search.jl` is ever
pushed to degrees where it struggles, this is the first thing to reach for — the measurement
above says the conditioning gain is real, only misapplied here.

Memoising the Gram entries is worth recording separately: `G` has `nc²` entries but only the
distinct exponent sums are distinct moments — 2583 against 123904 at degree 125 — and each is
an exact rational sum over `O(i²)` terms. Computing them per entry rather than per value
would have dominated everything else.
